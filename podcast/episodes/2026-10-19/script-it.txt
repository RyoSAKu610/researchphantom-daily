Il primo articolo: «Formally Verified Autoresearch for Analyzing the Expressivity of Multi-Head Attention». Gli autori sono Karthik Viswanathan, Plamen Dimitrov, Stefano Rocca, et al. Multi-head attention is a central component of the transformer architecture, yet how its expressivity depends on the number of heads remains poorly understood. We study a one-layer, attention-only transformer with additive positional embeddings and define the head complexity of a Boolean function as the minimum number of attention heads required to represent it. We derive an exact linear-fractional normal form for head complexity, prove a general threshold-degree lower bound, and give an explicit ten-bit function for which this bound is strict. For symmetric Boolean functions, we independently show that head complexity equals the number of times the function changes value between consecutive Hamming-weight levels, even when positional embeddings are allowed.

Il secondo articolo: «Repair, Retry, and the Cost of Hindsight: Verifier Feedback in Formal Theorem Proving». Gli autori sono Jack Li, Sophia Li. Verifier-guided self-repair conditions a model on feedback from failed attempts before it tries again, without weight updates. Yet two findings in prior work are in tension: verifier feedback can improve repair of failures, but self-repair itself can provide little-to-no advantage over independent resampling. We reconcile these findings in Lean theorem proving through controlled one-step experiments that hold the preceding failure fixed while varying whether the failed proof is shown, whether the compiler error corresponds to that failure, and the retry instruction. We test frozen Qwen3.6-27B, a general-purpose model, and DeepSeek-Prover-V2-7B, a specialized prover, on difficult MiniF2F problems. We find two opposing effects.

Il terzo articolo: «At the Limits of Computability: Steps Towards Autonomous AI Discovery on the Busy Beaver Frontier». Gli autori sono Marco Dos Santos, Vivek Veeriah, Alex Havrilla, et al. Recent breakthroughs in AI for mathematics---ranging from Olympiad medal performances to resolving long-standing open conjectures---have rapidly expanded what automated systems can achieve. Yet these successes primarily concern predefined, human-formulated problems, leaving open how AI systems can autonomously uncover mathematical structure and formulate new questions. To push beyond static challenges, we explore the Busy Beaver (BB) domain, an uncomputable frontier that is fundamentally open-ended. Because no algorithm can compute the Busy Beaver number for every , this domain provides a natural setting for sustained exploration through mathematical conjecture and proof.

Il quarto articolo: «Lean Graph Search for Formal Theorem Proving». Gli autori sono Yihan Xu, Jia-Chen Gu, Junyi Zhang, et al. Retrieving relevant lemmas is crucial for formal theorem proving, enabling provers to build machine-checkable proofs from established mathematical results. Recent retrieval systems match library declarations to a target theorem or its intermediate proof steps, but make limited use of multi-hop dependencies among declarations. These dependencies can reveal supporting lemmas that are not necessarily lexically similar to the target theorem but recur in proofs of related problems. To this end, we propose LeanGraphSearch, a graph-based retrieval method for Lean 4 that constructs a graph from Mathlib's forward dependencies and traverses it to uncover supporting lemmas through multi-hop dependency chains.

Il quinto articolo: «Learning Lean Feedback for Efficient Proof-Candidate Evaluation on GPUs». Gli autori sono Lazar Milikic, Etienne Bamas, Emmanuel Abbe, et al. Training language models for formal theorem proving requires large numbers of verifier calls. Slow checks and timeouts often leave GPUs waiting and withhold rewards from potentially correct proofs. We train neural evaluators to predict Lean 4 verdicts and compiler feedback on GPUs using 4.84M labelled programs. We compare two validity classifiers with a feedback model that annotates proof attempts with predicted goal states and error messages. A two-stage evaluator that screens candidates with a classifier and scores its positive predictions with the feedback model reaches 87.8% accuracy in predicting whether Lean accepts a candidate proof, evaluated on a 68,932-program benchmark collected from public provers.
